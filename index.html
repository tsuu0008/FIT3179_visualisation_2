Victoria · Finding a walk that fits

Walking the
uneven map

You have a free afternoon and want to go for a walk. Victoria’s recorded walking routes stretch for nearly 876 kilometres, so there seems to be plenty of choice. But which walk is close enough, manageable enough and right for the people coming with you?

For everyday walkers, occasional explorers and families in Victoria. You do not need to be an experienced bushwalker to follow this story. Start with somewhere to go, discover why a short walk can still ask a lot of you, then narrow the choices around your time and access needs. The question throughout is simple: what turns a line on a map into a walk that fits your day?

This story follows all 195 walking routes in Victoria’s official Recreation Tracks records, checked on 23 October 2026. It covers that published network, rather than every local footpath. The records include 20 routes marked closed, so the maps describe the recorded network rather than current availability. [1] Sources and methods.

01

First, find somewhere to go

Where you start changes the choice

A day out begins with a place. Yet those 195 routes are spread unevenly across Victoria: some areas offer many possibilities to explore, while others have far fewer in this network. Before choosing a distance or a difficulty, it helps to see where the choices gather.

Know what a grade can tell you

What does the recorded grade tell us?

The charts keep the walking-grade groups supplied with these records. The five cards summarise what is actually recorded in each group. A grade is a starting point: compare the route’s terrain, distance, time and experience advice before choosing.

Most of these walks gather in the east

More than three-quarters of the routes sit east of a north–south line through Melbourne. Murrindindi and East Gippsland alone account for 60 of the 195 walks—almost one in three.

For someone choosing a destination, that concentration means more routes to compare in those areas. Elsewhere, the list is smaller. The promise of hundreds of kilometres becomes more useful when you bring it down to a place you could visit.

From the network to a local choice

Local choices differ

Murrindindi offers 32 recorded routes, giving you a much longer list to explore than many other council areas. Even there, choosing the area is only the beginning: a short stroll and a long outing can sit within the same boundary.

Think of a council area as a first search area, rather than a finished plan. Finding several walks together gives you alternatives to compare. Their lengths and grades help you decide which deserve a closer look before you settle on one.

Explore locally: choose a council, or return to All Victoria. Coloured lines identify grades; grey means Unknown and violet means Unclassified. Rings locate an area label and a highlighted route, rather than trail entrances. “LGA” is another name for a council area.

Trail concentration · 25 km bins

Several choices may be neighbours

One 25 km-wide square contains the midpoints of 13 routes, all in Murrindindi. That small part of the map holds about one in fifteen of the walks in this story.

A council name can cover a large area. Its walks may gather in one corner instead of being spread throughout it. Looking closer helps turn “somewhere in this region” into a more focused place to explore, with several routes nearby to compare.

Read the squares: each covers 25 km × 25 km. Darker blue means more route midpoints; numbers label squares with at least four. A long route can extend beyond the square containing its midpoint.

The count uses one midpoint per route. The position and size of the squares affect which routes are grouped together.

Those clusters raise a wider question: do the places with the most people also have the most walking opportunities? A large total can look generous until you consider how many people live alongside it. Population figures from the Australian Bureau of Statistics help put the regional differences into perspective. [2]

The longest network is not the whole picture

Yarra Ranges has the most recorded walking distance: about 154 km. Murrindindi has less, about 132 km, but also far fewer residents. When population is taken into account, Murrindindi moves into first place.

For a resident, a regional total is only part of the picture. Murrindindi’s smaller network sits alongside just 15,648 people. Yarra Ranges’ longer network sits alongside a much larger population. The same two places tell a different story once the people living there enter the comparison.

Put places on the same footing

For every 100,000 residents, Murrindindi has about 844 recorded kilometres, compared with about 96 in Yarra Ranges—almost nine times as much. Using the same population size lets us compare councils of different sizes.

Paths are shared beyond the boundary

These kilometres are not a personal allowance: visitors walk here too, and residents can explore another council. The comparison shows how differently walking routes and homes are distributed. It does not tell us how busy a path is.

Where people live and paths gather do not always match

Whittlesea has the most residents among the 32 councils with these walking routes, yet comes 30th for walking kilometres relative to population. Cardinia is fourth for population and last for this measure.

A person living in a populous area cannot assume that this walking network offers a similarly large local choice. Murrindindi shows the reverse: a smaller population beside a relatively large network. Where people live and where these paths gather do not neatly match.

Read the positions: 1 means highest. Lines join each council’s position for population and walking kilometres per 100,000 residents. The 18 most populous councils are shown; positions are calculated among all 32 with walking records. A longer line means a larger gap between the two ranks. It does not measure kilometres or a change over time.

You now have a starting area, perhaps with several paths to explore. But a promising place is not yet a walk you would choose. The next decision happens along the path itself: will the ground, distance and experience needed suit you and your companions?

02

Next, look beyond the kilometres

A short walk can still be a demanding one

A short distance can be reassuring when you are planning an easy outing. Yet a few hundred metres of steep or rough ground can ask something very different of you from a level path. A walking grade is a useful first clue. It needs to be read alongside what the route says about its hills, surface and experience requirements.

One grade covers more than one kind of terrain

Short steep hills appear in both Grade 3 and Grade 4: 31 routes in one group and 20 in the other. A change in grade does not mean every feature of the ground changes with it.

If hills are the part of a walk that concerns you, the grade alone leaves a question unanswered. Two routes carrying the same grade can describe different ground; two carrying different grades can both include steep sections. Looking at the hills helps you choose around what matters to you.

Read the cells: rows describe the ground, columns show grades, and darker blue means more routes. There are 135 routes with both kinds of information. Missing grades are left out; Unclassified stays separate.

A higher grade is not simply a longer walk

The middle Grade 4 route is 2.5 km long, shorter than the middle Grade 3 route at 3.3 km. Moving up a grade does not simply add kilometres.

That matters when a shorter walk looks like the easier choice. Distance tells you how far you will go; the grade brings other demands into the decision. Reading both helps avoid mistaking a brief walk for a gentle one.

Read the points: the median is the middle distance when a group’s routes are ordered from shortest to longest. Counts beside the points show how many routes are in each group. Grade 1 has only two routes and Unclassified only one, so their middle distances describe very small groups.

Now bring that question down to an actual walk. Douglas Reserve Walk is just 400 metres, while Upper Yarra Walking Track stretches for 95 km. Both carry Grade 4 in these records. Sharing a grade clearly does not mean sharing the same kind of day out.

One grade, very different days out

Douglas Reserve Walk is listed as under an hour, with short steep hills and bushwalking experience recommended. Upper Yarra Walking Track is a multiple-day route for experienced bushwalkers. Both are Grade 4, but their lengths and time commitments are far apart.

The shorter option still asks you to consider hills and experience. The longer one asks for several days as well. This is where a route becomes more than a grade: you need to bring together how far it goes, how long it takes and what it asks of the people walking it.

Compare a walk: choose Douglas Reserve Walk or Upper Yarra Walking Track in the Trail menu. Follow the highlighted line across Grade, Distance, Duration and Terrain. The distance callout compares it with the median, or middle distance, of the matching walks. Long distances are compressed; use the kilometre labels. Read one column at a time: a higher line is not always a harder walk. The other columns are categories, so their spacing does not measure effort.

The short walk has not removed the need to think about experience. That is the next part of the choice: does a route welcome someone new to bushwalking, or does it ask for skills built on previous walks?

Experience becomes part of the choice

Of the 83 routes with short steep hills, 53 advise some bushwalking experience: 48 recommend it and another five call for experienced walkers.

For an occasional walker, that advice can change which route makes the shortlist. A distance you are comfortable with may still come with an experience requirement you did not expect. For a group, it is worth considering the person with the least experience as well as the keenest walker.

Read the bars: bar length counts routes, and coloured sections show recorded experience advice. Direct labels identify the larger segments. The comparison includes 188 routes with both terrain and experience recorded.

Hills are a substantial part of the choice

Routes described as having short steep hills contribute about 391 km—55% of the distance with both grade and terrain information. They are a substantial part of the walking on offer.

Someone looking for gentler ground is making a meaningful choice, rather than avoiding a rare exception. More than half the distance in this comparison belongs to routes described as having short steep hills. That describes the routes as a whole; it does not mean every metre of them is steep.

Read the rectangles: wider columns mean more walking kilometres in that terrain group. Colours show grades, and taller coloured blocks mean a larger share of that column’s kilometres. The area of each coloured rectangle therefore represents walking kilometres. Percentages above the columns show each terrain group’s share of the distance.

This comparison covers 706.52 km with grade and terrain information. The full walking network totals about 876 km.

You have moved from choosing a place to understanding the walk itself. A useful choice now has more than a name and a distance: its ground and experience advice make sense for you. The next step is to fit that walk into the time you have—and the needs of the people joining you.

03

Make it fit your day

Turn possibilities into a shortlist

Return to that free afternoon. You have somewhere in mind and a clearer idea of what you want underfoot. Now the choices have to work together: a manageable walk, enough time to enjoy it and the right conditions for the people coming along.

A region with many walks may offer you only a few

Return to Murrindindi, where the story began with 32 routes. If full or partial access is essential, that list becomes seven recorded possibilities. All seven are marked partial access.

The region has not changed, but the choices relevant to your group have. A long list of walks is useful only when some fit your needs. Comparing grade, distance and access together brings the regional promise closer to a day you could actually plan.

Build a shortlist: choose a grade and try the access filter. Larger symbols mean longer routes; colour and shape identify grade. Circled A marks the council with the most matches, and B the longest matching route. Symbols locate route midpoints, rather than entrances. Story figures describe the full dataset; these filters affect this chart only.

Think about everyone coming along

A companion who needs accessible facilities changes the starting question from “Which walk looks appealing?” to “Which walks describe the access we need?” Across Victoria, the filter keeps 15 routes marked full or partial access. The final chapter looks more closely at that small group.

A possibility still needs a closer look

Partial access may apply to only part of a route. Murrindindi’s seven possibilities therefore need more detail before one becomes a plan. The access description matters alongside the distance and grade, rather than being a final yes-or-no answer.

A brief outing has plenty of possibilities

More than half the walks—111 of 195—are listed as taking under an hour. Another 67 fall between one and four hours. Most belong to time categories that leave room for other plans in the day.

That is encouraging if you have an afternoon rather than a weekend. Yet a few routes ask for much more: five take over four hours, two are overnight and five span several days. Choosing a time category helps keep an appealing name on the map from becoming a bigger outing than you intended.

Read the bars: longer bars mean more walks in that time category. Labels show counts and percentages of all 195 routes. Five routes have no time recorded, shown in orange. Categories describe the published walking time, not an exact finish time or travel to the trail.

Most walks are short; a few change the scale

About three in four routes—151 of 195—are no longer than 5 km. The middle distance is just 2.2 km. The 95 km Upper Yarra Walking Track sits far beyond what most walks here ask in distance.

There is plenty to explore without choosing one of the longest routes. But remember the 400-metre Douglas Reserve Walk: short can still mean hills and experience advice. A distance that fits your afternoon becomes a useful choice only when the rest of the walk fits too.

Read the dots: each is one route. Long distances are compressed to give short walks more space; read the kilometre labels rather than judging by equal gaps. The dashed guide marks 5 km. Dots are nudged vertically to keep overlapping routes visible.

The afternoon walk is starting to take shape: somewhere you could visit, a distance and time you can allow, and demands that suit your group. One question still runs through the whole choice. Can everyone coming along use the path?

04

Make room for everyone coming along

The choice has to work for everyone

An inviting walk can still leave someone out. For a person who needs accessible facilities, the important starting point is whether the route describes those facilities at all. Across the whole network, only 15 routes record full or partial access. The generous total of nearly 876 km now looks very different through that person’s needs.

8.3%

15 of 180 rated routes record full or partial access.

The other 15 routes have no rating and are excluded from this percentage.

Partial access may cover only some sections or facilities. Four of these 15 routes are marked fully closed in the source snapshot; an access rating does not establish current availability.

A small starting list when access matters

Of the 180 routes with an access rating, 15 record full or partial access—about eight in every hundred. Another 15 routes have no rating and are left out of that percentage.

For someone who needs these features, access belongs near the start of choosing a walk. Finding a beautiful destination and a manageable distance is not enough if a companion cannot use the relevant section. The 15 routes offer a starting list for closer checking, rather than a promise that every one will suit every person.

Read the squares: imagine 100 routes with access ratings. About eight would record full or partial access, shown in orange. The actual count is 15 out of 180; the picture rounds 8.3% to eight squares.

For some walks, even the first questions remain open. Fourteen of the 15 routes without an access rating also lack a grade. Someone considering one of those routes has less help judging both its demands and its access. Clear information is part of making a choice possible.

What the story tells us

A usable walk has to fit a place, a day and a person.

Return to the free afternoon that began this story. You started with 195 routes and nearly 876 kilometres. The useful choice emerged through smaller questions: where could you go, what would the ground ask of you, how much time could you give it, and would it work for everyone coming along?

Most walks here are short, so a modest outing has many possibilities. Those possibilities gather unevenly, and a short distance can still come with steep ground or a need for experience. Where accessible facilities matter, the documented list becomes much smaller again.

The best walk for your day is not necessarily the longest, the nearest or the one with the lowest grade. It is one whose place, demands, time and access fit together for your group. That is how a line on Victoria’s uneven map becomes a walk you can begin to plan.

Data and method

What is included

The complete official WFS response retrieved on 23 October 2026 contains 366 recreation-track records. This website includes all 195 marked for walking (w_activity = Y), including MCG. The other 171 records are outside that walking-specific filter. Inclusion follows the published activity flag and does not certify each source record’s quality.

Grade 1–5 are display labels standardised from the published walking-grade field. Missing grades remain Unknown; values outside the five groups remain Unclassified. Original values are retained in the downloadable data and the exact conversion is documented in the reproduction notes. These labels report source groups; they do not certify a route against the public walking-grade guide.

What was verified

Distance, terrain, experience and accessibility match the current official source for every study route. Recorded track codes and duration categories were refreshed directly from that source. All 80 bundled population counts match column D of Table 2 in the ABS 2024–25 LGA workbook (population at 30 June 2025).

Geography and limits

Route lines retain every coordinate in the official walking export. Representative points are length-weighted route midpoints. Each full route is assigned to the council containing its midpoint, so cross-boundary routes are counted once. Council outlines come from the official ABS Local Government Areas 2025 GDA2020 shapefile. Midpoint assignments were checked against the full official polygons for all 195 routes; none changed. Boundaries are simplified by 100 metres for display. [3]

Original sources and verification evidence

The official Victorian WFS service supplied the complete RECWEB_TRACKS export. The original source codes are retained alongside the display-grade labels described above. Categorical duration is not converted to numeric hours. The original extraction history remains unknown.

Walking the
uneven map

Victoria’s promoted forest walks add up to nearly 872 kilometres. Yet a large network can offer very different choices depending on where a person lives, the demands of a route and the access it records.

Which walks can people actually use? Across 194 trails, the picture changes from regional abundance to individual suitability. This story concerns promoted state-forest recreation tracks; the everyday urban walking network sits outside its scope. [1]

01

Start with place

A rich network, unevenly distributed

Most of the network’s nearly 872 recorded kilometres lie within a regional forest setting. Before difficulty or duration matters, the first constraint is where those walking opportunities occur. [1]

A shared measure of difficulty

What does a walking-track grade mean?

Grade describes the overall difficulty of a walk—not just how steep it is. The Australian Walking Track Grading System considers experience, steps, gradient, track quality and signage; the most demanding criterion determines the final grade. [4] Grades use distinct colours throughout: Grade 1 blue, Grade 2 amber, Grade 3 teal, Grade 4 magenta and Grade 5 charcoal. Grey means unknown. Point charts also use different shapes, and grade numbers remain visible in axes, labels and tooltips. The selected parallel-coordinate profile keeps its grade colour and uses a thicker dashed line.

Grade 1 · AccessibleFlat, well-formed tracks with no steps. Suitable for assisted wheelchair users.
Grade 2 · EasyClearly formed tracks with gentle hills and occasional steps. No bushwalking experience required.
Grade 3 · ModerateSome short steep sections, rough surfaces or obstacles. Suitable for most ages with a reasonable level of fitness.
Grade 4 · DifficultLong, steep or rough tracks with limited signage. Bushwalking experience is recommended.
Grade 5 · Very difficultVery rough, steep or unmarked routes. Intended for highly experienced bushwalkers with specialised skills.

About the data: Australia’s standard grading system runs from Grade 1 to Grade 5. “Unknown” means no usable grade was recorded.

The forest network gathers east of Melbourne

Of the 194 trail midpoints, 152 lie east of 145°E, near Melbourne’s longitude. The promoted forest network is concentrated in one part of the state, rather than spread evenly across it.

For a walker, the statewide total is only a starting point. The first set of possible routes depends on place. Urban gaps here reflect the forest-focused dataset and do not mean that neighbourhood walking paths are absent.

Victoria overview · all 194 trails

Local choices differ

Murrindindi alone contains 32 of the 194 promoted trails. Regional concentrations such as this can offer a very different starting point from the statewide total.

Those kilometres also sit alongside very different populations. Local government areas (LGAs) provide a common geography for considering how recorded forest-walk supply relates to the number of residents. [2], [3]

A smaller population changes the supply story

Baw Baw has the largest recorded trail total, at 146.5 km. Murrindindi has slightly less, at 132.1 km, but its 15,648 residents give it the highest supply per resident: about 844 km per 100,000 people.

Baw Baw’s corresponding figure is about 233 km per 100,000 residents. The same network therefore has a different leader once population enters the picture. A large number of kilometres and a high per-resident supply describe different regional circumstances.

A small resident population helps produce Murrindindi’s high ratio. Visitors also use forest walks, and residents can travel beyond council boundaries, so this measure does not settle how easy a trail is to reach.

The population centres are not the supply leaders

Among areas with recorded trails, Whittlesea ranks first for population but 29th for supply per resident. Cardinia ranks third for population and last for supply, at 31st.

The places containing the most people are not the places leading this measure of forest-walk provision. That mismatch raises a question about reaching walking opportunities that a statewide kilometre total cannot answer.

People can walk beyond their own council boundaries, but the uneven distribution remains an important starting point. A statewide abundance of trail kilometres does not describe every local set of options. A potential route must then meet another test: the demands of the walk itself.

02

Understand the demands

A short walk can still be a demanding one

Once a place offers possible routes, difficulty becomes the next question. Grade 3 accounts for most trails, yet the same grade can include different terrain and distances. The network’s largest group is not one uniform walking experience.

One grade covers more than one kind of terrain

Grade 3 is the largest group, but it covers quite different conditions. Short steep hills are recorded for 69 Grade 3 trails, while another 38 have gentle hills.

A moderate grade leaves room for a gentler outing or repeated steep sections. The label narrows the field, but the terrain still changes the kind of day a walker can expect.

Higher grades often bring longer commitments

The median distance rises from 1.3 km at Grade 2 to 5.6 km at Grade 4. Grade 3 sits between them at 2.4 km, showing that the typical trail can remain fairly short even as difficulty increases.

Grade 5 reaches a 21 km median, but only two trails make up that group. It signals a very different commitment while offering much less evidence about what a typical difficult walk looks like.

A median distance describes the middle of a group, while an individual route can depart sharply from it. Grade, distance, duration and terrain combine into commitments that no single category can capture.

A shared grade can conceal a different commitment

Track Ninety Six is a Grade 5 walk with a 34 km distance and a recorded 48-hour estimate. Upper Yarra stretches to 95 km at Grade 4, yet has no recorded time.

Distance, duration and terrain do not settle into one simple difficulty ladder. A grade can summarise a route’s demands, but it cannot replace the rest of its profile. Even the longest route leaves an important planning question unanswered.

Terrain also brings expectations about preparation. The recorded experience advice shows how a walk’s demands extend beyond its length, while the distribution of kilometres reveals how much of the network has that character.

Experience becomes part of the choice

Flat and gentle routes mostly record no experience requirement. Short steep hills tell a different story: 48 of their 83 trails recommend bushwalking experience, and another five specify experienced bushwalkers.

The extra demand is therefore not confined to a handful of rare routes. Experience advice is a recurring feature of this part of the network, adding another consideration beyond the distance on a sign.

Short steep hills account for most kilometres

Among trails with known grade and terrain, short steep hills account for about 480.7 km, or 56.3% of recorded distance. Most of those kilometres fall within Grades 3 and 4.

This terrain is a major part of the network’s physical character. It matters both because it appears on many trails and because it accounts for more than half of the included walking distance.

Short steep hills feature heavily in both the trail records and their combined distance. Together with the experience recommendations, they explain why a manageable kilometre figure can still leave a demanding walk.

03

Bring the evidence together

Many trails, fewer suitable choices

A suitable walk has to fit a place, a day and a person. The network contains many short options, but distance alone cannot settle the time involved or the access available. The apparent range of choices becomes more specific as those needs come together.

The shortlist shrinks when access matters

The network offers many short walks, but the available pool changes when difficulty and recorded access enter the decision. Regional abundance does not guarantee a match for a particular walker.

Murrindindi makes the distinction concrete. Its 32 trails include seven with partial access and five with no rating. Even in the area leading per-resident trail supply, the records describe a narrower set of documented access options.

Short walks dominate the network

Across the full dataset, 150 of 194 trails (77%) are 5 km or shorter. Upper Yarra’s 95 km route is exceptional. The headline distance of the network includes both brief outings and much larger undertakings.

A moderate grade still leaves an access gap

Of 115 Grade 3 trails, four record full or partial access and nine have no access rating. The largest difficulty group offers many routes, but documented access is concentrated in a much smaller set.

Similar distances can mean very different days

Track Ninety Six records 34 km and 48 hours. The Great Dividing Trail is longer, at 38.7 km, but records only 12 hours. A similar distance can therefore come with a very different time commitment.

Estimated time generally rises with distance, yet these exceptions show how much a single kilometre figure can hide. The records establish the contrast; they do not establish its cause.

Most choices sit at the short end

The full-network median is just 2.2 km. Most trails sit well below Upper Yarra’s 95 km extreme, so a few long routes stretch the distance story far beyond the usual outing.

For many walkers, distance may be the easiest requirement to satisfy. Where a route lies, the terrain it involves and its recorded access can make the more decisive difference.

The network’s many short walks can make distance seem like the simplest part of the decision. Duration adds uncertainty: 45 trails have no recorded time estimate. Accessibility narrows the documented choice further, even where a trail’s grade and length appear suitable.

04

Return to the whole network

Recorded accessibility narrows the choice

The final constraint changes the scale of the opportunity. Across all 194 trails, only 15 record full or partial access. A network can contain substantial walking distance while documenting relatively few options for people who need those provisions.

8.4%

Only 15 of the 179 trails with an access rating record full or partial access. The documented share is small even before a walker’s location and other needs enter the decision.

A further 15 trails have no rating. Their missing information leaves part of the network’s accessibility unresolved.

Recorded access reaches only a small share

The Grade 3 group makes this gap concrete: its 115 routes include only four with full or partial recorded access. Another nine have no rating. The access story therefore concerns both the options documented and the questions the records leave unresolved.

What the story tells us

Usable choice depends on place, demands and access.

Victoria’s promoted forest walks offer substantial regional opportunities, but the choices are uneven. A useful route must be reachable, suit the walker’s abilities and time, and meet their access needs. For planners, the findings point beyond the statewide kilometre total to the options available in particular places. The quality of access information is part of that picture: an unrated trail leaves a practical question unanswered.

Data and method

What is included

The bundled snapshot contains 194 walking-track records attributed to State Forest Recreation Tracks [1]. Population uses the ABS 2024–25 release [2]; the boundary source is listed separately [3]. Live source pages may have changed since the supplied extract. The original extraction date and the bundled boundary vintage are unverified. The official export could not be fetched during this revision; the 194 observations were retained, while plotting tables and totals were rebuilt reproducibly. Verification record.

Processing

The supplied inputs contain walking records with standardised grade labels and spelling variants. Each trail is assigned to the LGA containing its midpoint, so a route crossing several areas is counted once. The retained geometry was already simplified for display; its original simplification method is unavailable. All current derived tables can be rebuilt with the data preparation script.

Scope of the comparisons

Supply measures recorded kilometres per 100,000 residents, not all walking infrastructure. The rank comparison shows the 18 most populous areas among those with recorded trails; ranks are calculated within that recorded-trail group. The headline statistics describe the full snapshot. Missing attributes are excluded only where the relevant comparison requires them.

Data and display notes

¹ Distance and time in the parallel coordinates, and distance in the strip plot, use square-root scales. Grade and terrain are ordered categories, with no equal-effort interpretation. Missing parallel-coordinate values occupy a separate lane rather than zero. The profiles compare attributes of a trail, not a journey through time.

The heatmap includes 186 trails with known grade and terrain; the experience bars include 187 with known terrain and experience. The mosaic uses kilometres with known grade and terrain. Its block values are rounded, so displayed totals can differ slightly from the underlying values.

The time comparison includes 149 positive recorded estimates. The access percentage excludes 15 unrated trails; the waffle rounds 8.4% to eight coloured cells out of 100. Access ratings come from the source data and are not an independent accessibility audit.

Route geometry represents the network in the overview; the circle map uses representative trail midpoints, which are not necessarily entrances. Interactive selections apply to their own chart; the surrounding narrative reports the full snapshot or explicitly named examples.

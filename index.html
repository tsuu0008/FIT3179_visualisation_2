Victoria’s walking-track records add up to nearly 876 kilometres in this official snapshot. Yet the choices available depend on place, terrain, time and recorded accessibility.

Which walks can people actually use? Follow 195 named trails from the regional network to individual suitability. The snapshot includes every record marked for walking in the Recreation Tracks source; Victoria’s everyday footpath network sits outside this dataset. [1]

02

Understand the demands

A short walk can still be a demanding one

Distance, terrain and preparation describe different parts of a walk. The official export records C1–C6 and one literal code “3” for MCG. These are preserved as source codes throughout this story; they have not been converted into public walking Grades 1–5. A missing code remains unknown.

Read the source carefully

Track codes and public walking grades are different labels

Source code is a recorded category, not an equal-step difficulty or effort scale. There is no code for 56 of the 195 routes. Use the terrain and experience fields alongside it. Blue, amber, teal, magenta, charcoal and violet identify C1–C6; brown identifies the literal source value “3”, and grey means unknown. The overview uses blue for every route. MCG’s “3” is kept separate from C3.

Planning background: what public walking Grades 1–5 mean

The Australian Walking Track Grading System considers experience, gradient, steps, track quality and signage. Grade 1 describes relatively level, well-formed routes; Grade 2 involves gentle hills; Grade 3 can include short steep sections; Grades 4 and 5 call for greater bushwalking experience and preparation. These public grades are background guidance and are not assigned to this project’s source codes. [4]

One code still covers different terrain

C4 is the largest recorded source-code group, with 56 trails. Twenty of those record short steep hills.

Cell colour and labels count trails with both a source code and terrain recorded: 135 routes. Unknown values are excluded from this comparison, rather than assigned a difficulty grade.

A source code does not form a simple distance ladder

The median is 1.5 km for C2, 3.25 km for C3, 2.5 km for C4 and 7.36 km for C5.

Each point shows a group median and sample size. C1 has only two routes and C6 only one, so those medians describe very small groups. Code order should not be read as a fixed increase in distance.

A group median cannot describe a particular day out. A route’s distance, duration category and terrain must be read together.

A shared code can conceal a different commitment

Upper Yarra Walking Track stretches to 95 km. Track Ninety Six is 34 km. Both record “multiple days”, with no numeric hour estimate in the verified export.

Read each axis separately: distance uses square-root spacing. Source code, duration category and terrain occupy ordered display positions; their spacing is not an equal-effort or elapsed-time scale.

“Not recorded” is a separate lane, rather than zero. Duration categories come directly from the source; they are not converted to invented numeric hours. A named route outside the chosen filters produces no highlighted profile.

Experience becomes part of the choice

Of 83 routes with short steep hills, 48 recommend bushwalking experience and another five specify experienced bushwalkers.

Bar length counts trails, and the coloured sections show the recorded experience advice. The comparison includes 188 routes with both terrain and experience recorded.

Steep terrain also accounts for substantial distance

Short steep hills account for 390.82 km of the 706.52 km included in this source-code and terrain comparison: 55.3%.

Rectangle width shows a terrain’s share of included kilometres. Coloured height shows the source-code mix within it, so rectangle area represents distance. Records without a code or terrain are excluded here; the full study still contains 875.905 km.

A short distance can still involve steep terrain and experience requirements. Those attributes explain the commitment more directly than a code on its own.

What the story tells us

Usable choice depends on place, demands and access.

A large kilometre total does not guarantee a suitable local walk. Distribution and population reveal different supply patterns. Terrain, distance, duration categories and experience advice describe individual demands. Recorded access then narrows the documented options. For walkers and planners, the useful question is which routes fit people’s needs—and where the available information leaves that question open.

Data and method

What is included

The complete official WFS response retrieved on 23 October 2026 contains 366 recreation-track records. This website includes all 195 marked for walking (w_activity = Y), including MCG. The other 171 records are outside that walking-specific filter. Inclusion follows the published activity flag and does not certify each source record’s quality.

What was verified

Distance, terrain, experience and accessibility match the current official source for every study route. Recorded track codes and duration categories were refreshed directly from that source. All 80 bundled population counts match column D of Table 2 in the ABS 2024–25 LGA workbook (population at 30 June 2025).

Geography and limits

Route lines retain every coordinate in the official walking export. Representative points are length-weighted route midpoints. Each full route is assigned to the council containing its midpoint, so cross-boundary routes are counted once. The simplified council boundaries are retained and their vintage remains unverified.

Original sources and verification evidence

The official Victorian WFS service supplied the complete RECWEB_TRACKS export. Raw source codes C1–C6 and the literal value “3” are retained without conversion to public walking grades. Categorical duration is not converted to numeric hours. The original extraction history remains unknown.

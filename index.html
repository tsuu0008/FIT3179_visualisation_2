Walking the
uneven map

Hundreds of kilometres of forest trails offer many ways to explore Victoria. But a walk must be somewhere you can reach, demanding enough to enjoy, and suitable for your needs.

Which walks can people actually use? Follow the network from location to difficulty, build a shortlist, then examine recorded accessibility. This story covers promoted state-forest recreation tracks, rather than every walking path in Victoria. [1]

How to read the colours

What does a walking-track grade mean?

Grade describes the overall difficulty of a walk—not just how steep it is. The Australian Walking Track Grading System considers experience, steps, gradient, track quality and signage; the most demanding criterion determines the final grade. [4]

Grade 1 · AccessibleFlat, well-formed tracks with no steps. Suitable for assisted wheelchair users.
Grade 2 · EasyClearly formed tracks with gentle hills and occasional steps. No bushwalking experience required.
Grade 3 · ModerateSome short steep sections, rough surfaces or obstacles. Suitable for most ages with a reasonable level of fitness.
Grade 4 · DifficultLong, steep or rough tracks with limited signage. Bushwalking experience is recommended.
Grade 5 · Very difficultVery rough, steep or unmarked routes. Intended for highly experienced bushwalkers with specialised skills.

About the data: Australia’s standard grading system runs from Grade 1 to Grade 5. “Unknown” means no usable grade was recorded.

Understand the demands

A short walk can still be a demanding one

Once location narrows the options, difficulty becomes the next question. Grade summarises several demands, so two walks of the same grade can still differ in terrain and distance. The grade guide above introduced the shared colour language used throughout this story.

Grade 3 spans varied terrain

Grade 3 appears across both gentle hills and short steep sections, so grade alone does not describe the whole experience.

How to read: darker cells represent more trails.

Typical distance rises with grade

Median distance rises from 1.3 km at Grade 2 to 5.6 km at Grade 4. Grade 5 has a 21 km median, based on only two trails.

How to read: dots show median distance; n is the trail count.

Grade 3 spans several terrain types, while the grade-level medians show that a typical walk can remain short even at a higher grade. Distance therefore adds useful information to grade rather than replacing it. The parallel coordinates below follow individual trails across grade, distance, estimated time and terrain. Watch how lines starting at the same grade fan out: one grade can describe several different commitments.

Return to the whole network

Recorded accessibility narrows the choice

The shortlist shows why suitability involves several constraints at once. Mobility access adds another: low grade or short distance alone cannot establish whether a trail meets a person’s needs. Returning to the full dataset, only 15 trails record full or partial disabled access.

What the story tells us

More kilometres do not automatically mean more usable choices.

The network offers substantial regional walking opportunities, but location, terrain, distance and recorded access shape who can use them. For walkers, the useful choice comes from combining these attributes. For planners, the findings raise questions about maintaining regional routes, connecting people to them, and improving accessibility and its documentation. Answering those questions requires travel information and walking networks beyond this dataset.

Data and method

What is included

The bundled snapshot contains 194 promoted walking tracks from Recreation Tracks [1]. Population uses the ABS 2024–25 release [2]; the boundary source is listed separately [3]. Live source pages may have changed since the supplied extract.

Processing

The supplied project filters walking records, standardises grade labels and spelling variants, and assigns each trail to the LGA containing its midpoint. A route crossing several areas is therefore counted once. Geometry is simplified for display.

Reading the results

Supply is recorded kilometres per 100,000 residents, rather than total walking infrastructure. Rankings compare the areas with recorded trails. Missing attributes stay missing. Each set of controls affects one plot only: the regional detail, supply map, trail map, distance plot, time plot or parallel coordinates. The statewide overview and chapter summaries always describe the full dataset. Route locations are represented by geometry in the overview and by midpoints in the circle map.
